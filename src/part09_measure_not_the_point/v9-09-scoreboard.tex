\section{Where I would bet}
\markboth{Where to bet}{}

Three directions, ranked by how likely I think each is to produce something genuinely new within a
decade.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\footnotesize,
  b/.style={rounded corners=4pt,draw=#1,line width=1.3pt,fill=#1!8,inner sep=9pt,
            text width=5.05cm,align=left,minimum height=4.95cm}]
 \node[b=measure] (a) at (-5.7,0)
   {\textbf{\color{measure}\normalsize 1 · Generative samplers of Boltzmann measures}\\[5pt]
    Normalizing flows, diffusion and flow matching used not to \emph{predict a structure} but to
    \emph{sample a distribution} with correct statistical weights.\\[5pt]
    {\scriptsize \textbf{Why:} it attacks $Z$, the common villain in all three of your problems.}\\[4pt]
    {\scriptsize \textbf{Horizon:} usable output in $\sim$2 years.}};
 \node[b=bio] (b) at (0,0)
   {\textbf{\color{bio}\normalsize 2 · Transfer-operator learning for kinetics}\\[5pt]
    Koopman theory is mature; VAMPnets showed it can be learned from short simulations; almost nobody
    in pharmacology uses it.\\[5pt]
    {\scriptsize \textbf{Why:} predicting $k_{\text{off}}$ rather than $\Delta G$ would change drug
    discovery more than better docking would.}\\[4pt]
    {\scriptsize \textbf{Horizon:} usable output in $\sim$2 years.}};
 \node[b=alert] (c) at (5.7,0)
   {\textbf{\color{alert}\normalsize 3 · Open quantum systems in warm structured environments}\\[5pt]
    Proton tunnelling in DNA, radical-pair magnetoreception and enzymatic hydrogen tunnelling are the
    \emph{same} mathematical problem in three biological costumes.\\[5pt]
    {\scriptsize \textbf{Why:} it is the only one where the mathematics genuinely does not exist.}\\[4pt]
    {\scriptsize \textbf{Horizon:} decade-scale. Highest risk, highest novelty.}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{plain}[breakable=false]
If a student wants to \emph{do mathematics}, send them to number 3. If a student wants to
\emph{change what the lab can measure within a PhD}, send them to number 1 or 2. Both are honourable;
confusing them wastes years.
\end{plain}

\begin{critbox}[title={And the caution I keep repeating, because it keeps being true}]
For most of what a molecular biology lab does day to day, the binding constraint is still
\textbf{measurement}, not mathematics. Part VIII made that case with your own sequencing data: half
the errors came from four moments on a flow cell, and no new mathematics was required to find them.

\textbf{New mathematics is the right answer to a specific, well-posed, blocked calculation.} It is
almost never the right answer to ``our results are noisy''.
\end{critbox}
