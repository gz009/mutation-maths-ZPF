\begin{plain}
Imagine grading 7{,}028 exam papers and finding that four of them account for nearly 40\,\% of all the
mistakes in the whole pile. You would not respond by grading every paper four more times. You would
find those four papers. That is the entire argument of this document.
\end{plain}

\begin{alertbox}[title={The part that should worry us: the confidence was wrong, not just the base}]
On tile 1113 at cycle 85, \textbf{99.3\,\% of bases were wrong}. Of those wrong bases, \textbf{658
carried a reported quality of Q39} --- the instrument's claim that it makes one mistake per 8{,}000
bases.
\[
\underbrace{\text{claimed error rate } 1.3\times10^{-4}}_{\text{Q39}}
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
\underbrace{\text{actual error rate } 0.993}_{\text{measured}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\textbf{7{,}600}\times\text{ over-confident}
\]
\textbf{Every downstream tool believed that Q39.} Trimming kept those bases. The assembler weighted
them. The variant logic trusted them.
\end{alertbox}

\subsection{Why FastQC did not catch it}

FastQC's ``Per tile sequence quality'' module issued a single \texttt{WARN} for ``one tile-cycle out
of 28 $\times$ 251''. There were at least four catastrophic ones.
